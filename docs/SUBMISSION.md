## Project Title

CHRONOBREAK — When Speed Breaks Time

## Elevator Pitch

A procedurally animated journey through a collapsing clockwork universe, where acceleration transforms motion into stillness.

## Problem / Creative Challenge

Speed is usually depicted through faster movement, streaks, and motion blur. CHRONOBREAK explores the opposite visual idea: the faster the protagonist moves, the slower everything around it becomes.

The film runs on two clocks. An amber pulse accelerates continuously from about 0.8 to 23 units per second. Meanwhile the clockwork world's own time slows to 2.5% of normal, so gears stall, fracturing metal hangs in the air, and the viewer feels speed as everything else stopping. The final shot recasts the whole journey. The camera rises 420 units and the pulse's curved 3D path turns out to be a perfectly straight line: the **second hand** of a giant skeleton clock, which then ticks once.

## How We Built It

Everything in the film is generated by Python from two JSON files (`config/scene.json`, `config/render.json`). `python run.py build` rebuilds the full `.blend` from scratch in about 6 seconds, and `python run.py all` runs build → test → render → encode → verify → evidence.

- **Pure-NumPy core, tested outside Blender** (`animation_math.py`, `procedural.py`):
  - The pulse travels a cubic Bézier by **arc length**, using an 8,193-sample inverted chord-length table, so its speed is exactly what the motion curve says.
  - Its intrinsic speed follows `v(x) = v0 + a·x^p` (p = 2.2). The closed-form integral is normalised so the pulse arrives at exactly t = 20 s.
  - Three clocks (real, story and world time) are defined as rates and integrated on a fixed 240 Hz grid. World time slows to 0.025; story time gives a bullet-time beat that freezes even the pulse while the camera orbits it.
- **Distortion field** `D(x,t) = exp(−‖x − P(t)‖²/2σ²)` around the pulse:
  - slows nearby gears further;
  - decides which gears fracture (14 of 122);
  - drives amber emission in metal and shards.
- **Gears:** 10 procedural tooth-profile archetypes (12–120 teeth) laid out by a seeded rejection solver into **properly meshing trains**, with correct centre distances, ratios and tooth phase. The solver also guarantees clearance from the path, the camera and its sight lines, and the top-down view of the hand. Gears are linked duplicates of shared meshes.
- **Shards (Geometry Nodes):** 4,975 shards are points on one mesh carrying 12 seeded attributes. The `ChronoShards` node group evaluates the fragment equation per point: bounded burst envelope, drift, rotation, `D²` glow and fracture flash. It instances 8 convex-hull shard archetypes. The only keyed input is *World Time*.
- **Camera:** one continuous take with no cuts. Distance, azimuth, elevation, lens and f-stop are monotone-cubic (PCHIP) tracks in the clock's own frame. The rig blends into an exact top-down view on the clock axis for the reveal.
- **The reveal is geometric:** all Bézier control points lie in the hand's vertical plane, so the 3D path projects onto a straight radial line from the axis. A unit test checks this to 1e-9.
- **Look:** Eevee in Blender 5.2 with screen-space ray-traced reflections, motion blur, depth of field, keyed volumetric haze and AgX. All materials are procedural: concentric "circular graining" on titanium, brass and blued steel, plus an obsidian dial. The pulse's point light is the key light, with cold rim lights and a reveal softbox. The node-based compositor adds bloom, chromatic dispersion and a vignette.
- **Pipeline:** five render stages: QA stills, a full 640×360 animatic, 1080p benchmarks (48 vs 24 samples), the final render, then encode and verification. The final render uses resumable 40-frame chunks, one Blender process each, with automatic retries. Verification covers FFmpeg probe and full decode, SHA-256 checksums, a contact sheet, and a frame-difference scan for seams and flicker.

## Technical Innovation

- **Speed as the gap between two integrated clocks.** Every moving element reads either story time (the pulse) or world time (gears, shards, tick), so a single curve in `scene.json` retimes the whole universe consistently.
- **A frame-independent film.** Every keyed value is a pure function of the frame number, and cumulative tables are tested against independent per-frame integrals. As a result any frame renders identically in any process: re-rendered frames were bit-identical to the originals. That is what made crash-safe chunked rendering possible.
- **Procedural layout with guarantees.** The gear solver doesn't just scatter parts: it builds real meshing gear trains and rejects anything that would collide with the path, the camera or the reveal. That is how 122 gears can crowd the frame without ever hiding the pulse or the hand.
- **One analytic field drives behaviour and light.** `D(x,t)` slows gears, fractures them, and lights metal and shards, so cause and effect stay visible on screen.
- **Geometry Nodes as an evaluator, not a hand-built effect.** Python writes per-shard parameters as attributes and the node graph evaluates the motion equations live, so 4,975 instances are driven by one keyed number.
- **A designed reveal.** Planar control points turn a looping 3D flight into a straight clock hand: the twist is guaranteed by geometry and checked by a test, not cheated in compositing.

## Challenges

1. **Integrated GPU crash in long renders.** After 162 frames (≈45 min in one session) Blender crashed in the GPU compositor: a render-pass texture was null. Video memory on an integrated GPU is shared system RAM. *Resolution:* chunked rendering with a fresh Blender process per 40 frames, resume-by-file and retries. All 576 frames completed, and deterministic rendering meant chunks join seamlessly; the boundaries were checked with a difference scan.
2. **A defect that only existed at final settings.** The animatic ran without volumetrics, so in the final render a 1.1 MW reveal softbox was found scattering into the fading haze. It veiled the clock in grey and caused a visible luminance pop. *Resolution:* found by a full-sequence frame-difference scan and reproduced in a fresh process, which ruled out a chunk seam. Fixed with `volume_factor = 0` on that light; 106 frames re-rendered and re-verified.
3. **Mirrored lights in obsidian.** Large area lights showed up as white discs in the glossy dial plate. *Resolution:* the top light is off during the flight, its specular contribution is reduced, and the plate roughness and coat were retuned.
4. **Readability in a crowded mechanism.** Early stills had the centre arbor blocking the camera, bokeh blobs from shards near the lens, and white-outs from haze and glow. *Resolution:* camera and sight-line clearance in the layout solver, culling of shards near the camera, and retuned emission and density.
5. **Render budget on a laptop with no dedicated GPU.** Benchmarks showed 48 samples cost 36.5 s/frame against 19.8 s for 24 samples, with no visible difference at 100% crop. *Resolution:* the final uses 24 samples.
6. **Blender 5.2 API changes.** Geometry Nodes modifier inputs, the node-group compositor and slotted-action F-curves all differ from older tutorials, and each was handled explicitly (see `docs/TECHNICAL_WORKFLOW.md`).

Known tradeoffs are listed in `docs/QA_REPORT.md` §4, for example the harsh 2-frame whip when bullet time ends.
